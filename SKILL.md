# Unknown Unknowns

What a person knows about a new undertaking falls into three groups:

- **Known knowns**: what they already understand.
- **Known unknowns**: gaps they are aware of. They can search for these or ask about them.
- **Unknown unknowns**: things they do not know exist. They cannot ask, because they have no question to form.

Search and ordinary Q&A only serve the second group, because both need a question to start from. The third group is where newcomers lose the most, and insiders rarely volunteer it because to them it goes without saying. This skill exists to deliver that third group.

A good result is a short ranked list where most items make the person think "I had no idea that was a thing", and each one is concrete enough to act on today. Judge the list by that reaction, not by coverage. Thirty items is a failure: the three that matter get buried, and the person is back to not knowing what matters.

## Step 1: Pin down the situation

Blind spots are situational. The same goal produces a different list depending on:

- **Where**: country, region, city. Law, tax, and custom drive most surprises.
- **Scale and type**: five people or fifty, individual or company, hobby or commercial.
- **Timing**: what the next irreversible step is (signing, paying, launching, filing) and how soon.
- **Counterparty**: who is on the other side (landlord, employer, platform, agency, regulator).
- **What they have already done or decided.**

Pull these from the conversation first. Language, currency, city names, and product names are usually enough to infer the place.

If one missing fact would change most of the list and cannot be inferred, ask for it. Ask one question, two at most. Otherwise state the assumption in one line and proceed. People who ask "what am I missing" are usually close to a deadline, and a list built on a stated assumption is more useful to them than a questionnaire.

Assume facts about their situation, never choices they have yet to make. "You are in Hangzhou" is a safe assumption. "You will hire them as an employee, not a contractor" is not: that choice is exactly where a newcomer goes wrong, so it belongs in the list as an item. Whenever you catch yourself assuming they will do the right thing, you have found a candidate blind spot.

Also note what they evidently know already, from what they mentioned and how they phrased it. Telling someone what they just told you wastes their attention and signals the rest of the list is generic too.

## Step 2: Generate candidates through several lenses

Whatever comes to mind first is the generic beginner checklist, which is exactly what the person could have found alone. Work through these lenses to get past it. Collect 20 to 30 raw candidates before cutting anything.

- **Walk the whole timeline, including the end.** Newcomers think about getting in. Insiders think about getting out: termination, renewal, resale, handover, what happens when it fails.
- **Sit on the other side of the table.** What does the counterparty know that the newcomer does not? What is presented as fixed but is routinely negotiated? What do they earn that is not obvious?
- **Wrong defaults.** What does a newcomer silently assume that is false? ("The listed price is the price." "The contract is standard." "Approval is a formality.")
- **Order and lead time.** What must happen before something else can, and takes longer than expected? What cannot be redone once done in the wrong order?
- **Obligations that start silently.** What recurring duty begins the moment they take this step, such as filings, renewals, records to keep, or notices to give?
- **Money outside the headline number.** Fees, deposits, taxes, restoration costs, minimum terms, penalties. Also when the cash is due: how much must be paid on day one is often several times the monthly figure the newcomer budgeted around.
- **Thresholds and categories.** Rules that switch on at a headcount, a revenue level, a floor area, or a legal classification the newcomer does not know they fall into.
- **Inherited state.** What is already attached to the thing they are taking on from before they arrived? A previous occupant's registration on the address, a lien, an existing dispute, a prior owner's history, a dependency's licence. Newcomers inspect the thing as it looks today and not its record.
- **New exposure.** What does this step expose that was safe before? Personal liability that the company structure does not cover, private data that someone else can now see, an account or a person that becomes a single point of failure.
- **What normal looks like.** A newcomer cannot tell a good offer from a bad one because they do not know the usual range for price, size, or timeline. A benchmark lets them spot the outlier themselves.
- **The route they did not know existed.** Sometimes the blind spot is an alternative to the whole plan: a different structure, a programme they qualify for, a product that removes the problem. Signing the obvious option often closes the other.
- **Regret stories.** What do people write under "I wish someone had told me"? What makes practitioners wince when a first-timer describes their plan?

Then make a second pass for depth. The first pass tends to stop at what a well-read amateur knows. Ask: what would someone who handles this every week add, that even a careful person who had read three guides would not have? Those are usually procedural details, such as a check that is run before signing, a record that is looked up, or a clause that is always struck. A list needs at least a couple of items from this layer to be worth more than a search.

When the undertaking lives in files you can read (a codebase about to be launched, a contract draft, a plan document), read them first. A blind spot tied to a specific clause or file is worth more than a general one.

## Step 3: Filter for what is unknown to this person

Put each candidate through four tests:

1. **Would they have asked unprompted?** If yes, it is a known unknown. Drop it.
2. **Is it on every beginner checklist?** Then they have probably seen it. Keep it only if missing it would be severe and nothing they said suggests awareness.
3. **Does it apply to their situation?** Drop anything that only matters at a scale, place, or category they are not in.
4. **Can they act on it?** "Read the contract carefully" and "consult a professional" are not items. Either sharpen them into the specific clause to look for or the exact question to ask, or drop them.

This filter is the whole value of the skill. Being ruthless here is what separates this from a general guide.

## Step 4: Check the facts that carry weight

Insider knowledge is the worst place for a confident error, because a newcomer cannot tell it is wrong. For any item that rests on law, regulation, fees, thresholds, deadlines, or platform policy:

- If search tools are available, verify the top-tier items for the person's jurisdiction and give the source.
- If not, state the principle and mark it for local confirmation instead of quoting a figure from memory.
- Do not state a specific number, deadline, or statute you have not verified. A wrong threshold is worse than "there is a threshold, check what it is for your city".
- Attach a link only if you opened it and it supports the exact claim beside it. A link that turns out to be about something else damages trust in every other item.

Keep the checking proportionate. Spend it on the first tier and on anything with a number in it, with a handful of targeted lookups. Researching every item makes the person wait several minutes for a list they needed quickly.

Give every item a basis tag so the person knows how much to lean on it: **verified** (with source), **common practice**, or **varies, confirm with [whom]**.

## Step 5: Rank by the cost of not knowing

Order by, in priority: how irreversible the damage is, how large the loss is, how likely it is, and how soon the window to act closes. Then sort into three tiers:

1. **Cannot be undone**: discovered after signing, paying, or launching, with no cheap fix.
2. **Costs money or time**: recoverable, but painful.
3. **Smoother if you know**: saves friction or improves the outcome.

Keep 5 to 7 full items in total, with no more than 3 in the first tier. If more candidates survive the filter, put up to 4 of them under "Also worth a look" as one line each. Tier 1 is rare by nature. If everything lands there, the ranking is not doing its job.

## Output format

Write in the user's language. The whole reply should be readable in about three minutes, which is roughly 700 to 900 words in English or 1,500 to 2,000 characters in Chinese. This limit matters as much as the content: the person came because they could not tell what matters, and a long reply hands that problem back to them. When a draft runs over, remove the weakest items. Do not squeeze every item to fit.

Give first-tier items the full treatment. Items in the second and third tiers can be shorter: the fact, one sentence on the cost, and the action.

```
**Assuming:** [one line: place, scale, stage, and anything else you inferred]

## Cannot be undone

**1. [The blind spot stated as a fact, in one sentence]**
[Two or three sentences: why a first-timer does not think to ask, and what it costs when missed.]
→ Do now: [a specific check, or the exact question to put to the counterparty]
Basis: [verified + source | common practice | varies, confirm with X]

## Costs money or time
...

## Smoother if you know
...

## Also worth a look
- [one line each, optional]

## Safe to ignore for now
- [two to four things first-timers worry about that can wait, one line each]

## Words to know
- **[insider term]**: [one-line meaning]

## Who to ask, and what to ask
- [Kind of person]: "[exact question]"

## Not covered here
[One or two lines on what this list did not examine or cannot judge.]
```

Why each part is there:

- **Headline as a fact, not a topic.** "Insurance" is a topic and leaves the work to the reader. "The lease makes you pay to restore the space to bare condition when you leave" is a fact they can act on.
- **"Do now" as an exact question where possible.** Newcomers often do not know how to phrase the question so that the counterparty cannot dodge it. Hand them the wording.
- **Safe to ignore for now.** Not knowing what matters cuts both ways: newcomers also spend effort on things that can wait. Naming two to four of those frees attention for the first tier. Only list things that are truly deferrable in their situation, and say until when.
- **Words to know** (3 to 5 terms). A term is a key. Without the insider word for something, a person cannot search for it or recognise it in a contract. Choose terms that unlock further searching, not jargon for its own sake.
- **Who to ask.** The list is incomplete by construction. Point to one to three kinds of people who see many first-timers, with a question that makes them volunteer what they know, for example "What do people in my position usually get wrong in their first year?"
- **Not covered here.** State the limits plainly so the list is not mistaken for a guarantee.

## Example of one item

Goal: publishing a first app to the App Store as a small company.

> **1. Your developer account must be approved before you can submit, and for a company that can take days to weeks.**
> Enrolling as an organization requires a D-U-N-S number, which is requested separately and has its own wait. First-timers plan the launch date around finishing the code and find this out the week before.
> → Do now: start the organization enrollment today, in parallel with development.
> Basis: Apple's enrollment requirements. Confirm current lead times on the enrollment page.

Note what makes it work: the headline is a fact, the cost is concrete, the action can be taken today, and the basis says how far to trust it.

## Variations

**The user shares a plan and asks what is missing.** Use the plan as evidence of what they know in Step 3, and anchor each item to the part of the plan it affects.

**The user is clearly not a newcomer.** Raise the bar. Skip the third tier and the vocabulary section, and look for the blind spots experienced people have: the recent rule change, the assumption that used to be true.

**Legal, medical, or financial stakes.** Still give the list. Knowing what to raise with a professional is the point, and people in these situations often cannot afford to ask an open-ended question. Frame items as what to check and what to ask, keep the "Who to ask" section, and do not present the list as a substitute for advice on their specific case.

**The undertaking has legally required steps** (hiring, forming a company, importing goods, opening premises to the public). A ranked list of seven will leave some mandatory steps out, and skipping one is a violation whether or not it ranked among the costliest. After the tiers, add a section called "Required steps, in order" that lists every legally required step as one line with its deadline and no commentary. It does not count toward the item limit or the length target. Where an official checklist exists, check the list against it and link it.

**The goal is too broad** ("starting a business"). The list would be generic. Narrow it by asking what the next concrete step is, and build the list around that step.

## What to avoid

- Padding with basics to look thorough. Every obvious item lowers trust in the non-obvious ones.
- Writing a guide. The person did not ask how to do the thing. They asked what they cannot see.
- Vague cautions with no action attached.
- Specific figures or legal claims given from memory without a basis tag.
